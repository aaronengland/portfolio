Pricing Probability of Default (PD) Model

The Pricing PD model is a robust framework designed to evaluate credit risk by amalgamating deal structure features and historical credit data. By synthesizing these multifaceted elements, the model computes a PD score, quantifying the likelihood of a borrower defaulting on their obligations. Moreover, leveraging the predicted loss, calculated in conjunction with the PD score, this model forecasts the Expected Cumulative Net Loss (ECNL) over the tenure of the investment or loan. This nuanced understanding of potential losses empowers stakeholders to make informed decisions on risk mitigation strategies and pricing structures. Ultimately, armed with these insights, the pricing PD model facilitates the determination of optimal pricing strategies, aligning risk exposure with profitability and ensuring a judicious balance between risk and the success of a customer paying their obligation.

Leaky Feature Removal

All features within each data set were checked for percent missing, no variance, and/or redundancy. Any feature that was 80% missing or more, had no variance, or was redundant in any of the three data sets was removed from consideration in the model (with the exception of bankruptcy type). Next, all features were screened by name to ensure there were no ID, date, or unusable score features that were considered for the model. Features were also checked for target information leakage to ensure no features in consideration for modeling exposed information about the target that would artificially inflate its performance. Hyperparameter Tuning (Part 1)

The subsequent phase in feature selection involved fine-tuning the model's learning rate to optimize the Log loss on the validation dataset. The learning rate, a crucial hyperparameter in gradient descent optimization, dictates the magnitude of adjustments made during each iteration of model learning. It influences the speed at which the model assimilates information from the data. A higher learning rate accelerates learning but risks overshooting the optimal solution, whereas a lower rate may yield slower convergence but potentially more precise outcomes. The model's algorithm initially sets a default learning rate that adjusts with variations in the dataset's row count, yet remains constant with changes in the number of features. Consequently, the learning rate can be fine-tuned using the entire feature set to establish a baseline model, which can be further enhanced by feature reduction. To achieve optimal tuning, a set of 100 models was trained using learning rates spanning from 0.001 to 0.999. Model Building

The model construction phase operates in a cyclic manner, as illustrated in the diagram below, encompassing two primary segments: hyperparameter tuning and sensitivity analysis. Hyperparameter tuning aims to maximize performance on the validation dataset, whereas sensitivity analysis verifies that excluding any feature from the model does not enhance validation performance. Upon confirmation that no feature removal improves the validation score, the cycle concludes.

Hyperparameter Tuning (Part 2)

As the initial tuning job identified an optimal learning rate among the lower spectrum of values, the subsequent tuning job solely incorporated the initial 50 potential values. This approach optimizes computational resources, mitigates time expenditure, and minimizes the risk of suboptimal validation performance. Furthermore, by repeating the same learning rates, there is assurance that the model will either exhibit improvement or maintain its performance in this phase. Sensitivity Analysis

Following hyperparameter tuning, the constant optimal learning rate was maintained, and individual models were constructed, each excluding a distinct feature from the model. In cases where exclusion of a feature enhanced the validation score, the feature contributing the most to this improvement was removed. Compliance

Following the completion of the model building phase, several additional steps are essential for compliance purposes. These steps encompass creating Partial Dependence plots and a Disparate Impact Analysis, involving the comparison of model prediction distributions across distinct protected classes, such as race, gender, and age. Subsequently, a thorough examination is conducted to identify any model features that might serve as predictors of a protected class, assessing the individual impact of these features on model performance. Lastly, there is a crucial mapping process associating adverse action reasons with specific features, facilitating the provision of adverse action reasons to applicants upon request.

Assigning a monetary value to differences in AUC score was estimated using the following methodology: Assuming the population approval rate is 30% and Prestige averages 25,000 applications per month a sample of 25,000 values were generated from a binomial distribution with P = 0.30. Given that AUC can be approximated as the average of sensitivity and specificity, a large sample of 1000 possible combinations for a specific AUC value were randomly generated. Given the number of positive and negative simulated defaults the number of expected true positive and true negative results given each sensitivity and specificity value, respectively, were estimated. Then the number of false positive and false negative could be solved for by subtracting true negative from total negative or true positive from total positive, respectively. Given our business we would expect to make $0 from a true positive (correctly identified a bad loan), $2,500 from a true negative (average profit), -$2,500 from a false positive (missed average profit), and -$10,000 from a false negative (expect to lose 50% of amount financed, typically $20,000 per loan). These monetary values were multiplied by the number of cases for each confusion matrix simulated from a specified AUC value. An added complication was assuming a 12% capture rate for approved loans, which means both true negatives and false negatives got multiplied by 0.12.

The total monetary value was averaged across all simulated combinations of sensitivity and specificity for a specified AUC value. By running the procedure across a range of AUC values it was determined that a 0.001 difference in AUC translated to $55,000 in loss.

Assigning a monetary value to differences in RMSE was estimated using the following methodology. Given that “risk” is defined in deviation from an expected outcome, and assuming 900 financed loans per month at $18,000 per loan 0.01 RMSE translated to $162,000 in increased risk.

Disparate Impact Analysis

This analysis involves evaluating the outcomes of these policies across different demographic groups (i.e., race, age, and gender). The disparate impact analysis for this model involved comparing the distributions of predictions between and among different protected groups. Accuracy refers to the ratio of correctly predicted instances to the total instances in the dataset. It's a common metric used to evaluate classification models. Interpreting accuracy involves understanding the proportion of correct predictions made by the model out of all predictions. For instance, if a classifier has an accuracy of 80%, it means that 80% of the predictions made by the model were correct. However, accuracy might not always be the best metric, especially when dealing with imbalanced datasets where one class heavily outweighs the others. In such cases, other metrics like precision, recall, or F1 score might provide a more comprehensive understanding of the model's performance.

Examples: Accuracy is suitable for evaluating a spam email classifier when the dataset contains an almost equal number of spam and non-spam emails. Similarly, in a medical diagnosis task with a balanced representation of patients with and without a particular condition, accuracy can be a suitable metric to assess the model's performance.

F1 score is a metric that combines precision and recall into a single value. It is particularly useful in binary classification settings, where there are two classes to predict. Interpreting the F1 score involves understanding its balance between precision (the proportion of correctly predicted positive observations among all predicted positives) and recall (the proportion of correctly predicted positive observations among all actual positives). The F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. It provides a single score that considers both false positives and false negatives, aiming for a balance between precision and recall. A higher F1 score indicates better model performance, where precision and recall are both high. Conversely, a lower F1 score indicates that either precision or recall, or both, are low.

Examples: The F1 score is valuable when dealing with imbalanced datasets, such as in fraud detection, where the number of fraudulent cases is significantly lower than non-fraudulent ones. Additionally, in text classification, like sentiment analysis, where precision and recall are equally important for balanced performance, the F1 score provides a balanced assessment.

Log loss, also known as logarithmic loss or cross-entropy loss, is a common evaluation metric for binary and multiclass classification models. It measures the performance of a classifier by quantifying the difference between predicted probabilities and the actual true values. Interpreting log loss involves understanding how well the predicted probabilities align with the actual classes. The lower the log loss, the better the model's predictions align with the true values. Log loss penalizes incorrect predictions more severely when they are confidently wrong, meaning when the predicted probability diverges significantly from the actual class label. Log loss is a logarithmic function, and its values range from 0 to infinity. A log loss closer to 0 indicates better performance, while a higher log loss indicates poorer performance. An ideal model would have a log loss close to 0, reflecting accurate and confident predictions.

Examples: Log loss is particularly useful in classification problems, such as predicting whether an email is spam or not, especially when evaluating the model's confidence in its predictions. Similarly, in medical diagnostics for identifying a specific disease from medical test results, log loss can measure the model's calibration and precision in assigning probabilities to the classes.

Precision is a metric that measures the accuracy of positive predictions made by the model. It quantifies the proportion of true positive predictions (correctly predicted positives) out of all instances predicted as positive (true positives + false positives). Interpreting precision involves understanding the fraction of correctly predicted positive instances among all instances predicted as positive by the model. A high precision score indicates that when the model predicts a positive outcome, it's often correct. Conversely, a low precision score suggests that the model incorrectly predicts positive outcomes more frequently, leading to more false positives. In applications such as medical testing or spam email filtering, high precision is crucial to minimize false positives and ensure the accuracy of positive predictions.

Examples: Precision is vital in scenarios where minimizing false positives is crucial, such as in medical diagnosis to ensure that predicted positive cases are accurate, reducing unnecessary treatments. In anomaly detection, like identifying cybersecurity threats, precision helps minimize false alarms, focusing on correctly flagging actual anomalies.

Precision-Recall AUC (Area Under the Curve) is a metric used to evaluate the performance of a machine learning classifier, particularly in binary classification problems. Unlike the traditional Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve, which plots the True Positive Rate against the False Positive Rate, the Precision-Recall curve illustrates the trade-off between precision and recall for different threshold values. The Precision-Recall AUC calculates the area under the Precision-Recall curve, summarizing the model's ability to balance precision and recall across various thresholds. A higher Precision-Recall AUC indicates a classifier that can achieve both high precision (few false positives) and high recall (few false negatives) simultaneously. Interpreting Precision-Recall AUC involves understanding the trade-off between precision and recall. A high Precision-Recall AUC signifies a model that can make precise predictions while also capturing a significant portion of positive instances (high recall) across different thresholds. It's especially useful when dealing with imbalanced datasets, where there's a substantial difference in the number of samples between classes, as it focuses on the positive class's performance.

Examples: Precision-Recall AUC is especially valuable when dealing with highly imbalanced datasets, like rare disease diagnosis, where correctly identifying the minority class is crucial. In fraud detection, where catching fraudulent transactions is the priority, Precision-Recall AUC helps assess the trade-off between precision and recall, providing insights into the model's performance across different decision thresholds.

Recall, also known as sensitivity or true positive rate, measures the model's ability to identify all relevant instances from the dataset, specifically the proportion of true positive predictions out of all actual positives. Interpreting recall involves understanding the fraction of correctly predicted positive instances out of all instances that were actually positive. A high recall score indicates that the model captures a large portion of the positive instances, minimizing false negatives. In applications such as disease diagnosis or anomaly detection, high recall is crucial to ensure that most actual positive cases are identified, even if it means accepting some false positives.

Examples: Recall is critical in scenarios where missing positive instances is highly undesirable, such as in medical testing for severe diseases where detecting all positive cases is essential, even at the expense of more false alarms. In fault detection in manufacturing, high recall ensures catching all defective products, prioritizing identifying actual faults over potential missed ones.

ROC AUC (Receiver Operating Characteristic Area Under the Curve) is a metric used to evaluate the performance of a machine learning classifier, typically in binary classification problems. It measures the area under the ROC curve, which plots the True Positive Rate (sensitivity) against the False Positive Rate (1 - specificity) at various threshold settings. Interpreting ROC AUC involves understanding the trade-off between true positive rate and false positive rate across different threshold values. A higher ROC AUC value (closer to 1) indicates better discrimination ability of the classifier between the positive and negative classes. Essentially, it demonstrates the model's capability to correctly distinguish between the two classes, with a higher value indicating a better-performing classifier. In simpler terms, a higher ROC AUC implies that the model can effectively differentiate between the positive and negative instances, achieving a better balance between true positives and false positives across different threshold settings.

Examples: ROC AUC is particularly useful when evaluating a model's performance across various decision thresholds, like in credit scoring, where balancing true positive rates with false positive rates is essential for assessing risk. In predictive maintenance for equipment failure, ROC AUC helps understand the trade-offs between correctly identifying impending failures and avoiding unnecessary maintenance interventions.
