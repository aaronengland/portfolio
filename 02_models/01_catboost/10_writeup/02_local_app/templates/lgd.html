Pricing Loss Given Default (LGD) Model

The Pricing LGD Model stands as a robust framework engineered to appraise credit risk, blending deal structure intricacies with historical credit insights. Within this model, the synthesis of diverse elements culminates in the computation of a LGD score — a metric that precisely gauges the anticipated portion of the financed amount susceptible to loss. Notably, in conjunction with the LGD score, the model utilizes the Probability of Default (PD) to forecast the Expected Cumulative Net Loss (ECNL) throughout the investment or loan's duration. This comprehensive understanding of potential losses equips stakeholders with invaluable insights, enabling informed choices regarding risk management strategies and pricing frameworks. Ultimately, the Pricing LGD model serves as a guiding compass, aiding in the determination of optimal pricing strategies. By harmonizing risk exposure with profitability, it ensures a prudent equilibrium between risk and reward, shaping judicious financial transactions.

Leaky Feature Removal

All features within each data set were checked for percent missing, no variance, and/or redundancy. Any feature that was 80% missing or more, had no variance, or was redundant in any of the three data sets was removed from consideration in the model (with the exception of bankruptcy type). Next, all features were screened by name to ensure there were no ID, date, or unusable score features that were considered for the model. Features were also checked for target information leakage to ensure no features in consideration for modeling exposed information about the target that would artificially inflate its performance. Hyperparameter Tuning (Part 1)

The subsequent phase in feature selection involved fine-tuning the model's learning rate to optimize the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) on the validation dataset. The learning rate, a crucial hyperparameter in gradient descent optimization, dictates the magnitude of adjustments made during each iteration of model learning. It influences the speed at which the model assimilates information from the data. A higher learning rate accelerates learning but risks overshooting the optimal solution, whereas a lower rate may yield slower convergence but potentially more precise outcomes. The model's algorithm initially sets a default learning rate that adjusts with variations in the dataset's row count, yet remains constant with changes in the number of features. Consequently, the learning rate can be fine-tuned using the entire feature set to establish a baseline model, which can be further enhanced by feature reduction. To achieve optimal tuning, a set of 100 models was trained using learning rates spanning from 0.001 to 0.999. Model Building

The model construction phase operates in a cyclic manner, as illustrated in the diagram below, encompassing two primary segments: hyperparameter tuning and sensitivity analysis. Hyperparameter tuning aims to maximize performance on the validation dataset, whereas sensitivity analysis verifies that excluding any feature from the model does not enhance validation performance. Upon confirmation that no feature removal improves the validation score, the cycle concludes.

Hyperparameter Tuning (Part 2)

As the initial tuning job identified an optimal learning rate among the lower spectrum of values, the subsequent tuning job solely incorporated the initial 50 potential values. This approach optimizes computational resources, mitigates time expenditure, and minimizes the risk of suboptimal validation performance. Furthermore, by repeating the same learning rates, there is assurance that the model will either exhibit improvement or maintain its performance in this phase. Sensitivity Analysis

Following hyperparameter tuning, the constant optimal learning rate was maintained, and individual models were constructed, each excluding a distinct feature from the model. In cases where exclusion of a feature enhanced the validation score, the feature contributing the most to this improvement was removed. Subsequently, the iterative tuning-sensitivity analysis cycle was reiterated. Termed as "Sensitivity Tuning", this process continued until no feature within the model exhibited enhanced performance upon removal. Compliance

Following the completion of the model building phase, several additional steps are essential for compliance purposes. These steps encompass creating Partial Dependence plots and a Disparate Impact Analysis, involving the comparison of model prediction distributions across distinct protected classes, such as race, gender, and age. Subsequently, a thorough examination is conducted to identify any model features that might serve as predictors of a protected class, assessing the individual impact of these features on model performance. Lastly, there is a crucial mapping process associating adverse action reasons with specific features, facilitating the provision of adverse action reasons to applicants upon request.

Assigning a monetary value to differences in AUC score was estimated using the following methodology: Assuming the population approval rate is 30% and Prestige averages 25,000 applications per month a sample of 25,000 values were generated from a binomial distribution with P = 0.30. Given that AUC can be approximated as the average of sensitivity and specificity, a large sample of 1000 possible combinations for a specific AUC value were randomly generated. Given the number of positive and negative simulated defaults the number of expected true positive and true negative results given each sensitivity and specificity value, respectively, were estimated. Then the number of false positive and false negative could be solved for by subtracting true negative from total negative or true positive from total positive, respectively. Given our business we would expect to make $0 from a true positive (correctly identified a bad loan), $2,500 from a true negative (average profit), -$2,500 from a false positive (missed average profit), and -$10,000 from a false negative (expect to lose 50% of amount financed, typically $20,000 per loan). These monetary values were multiplied by the number of cases for each confusion matrix simulated from a specified AUC value. An added complication was assuming a 12% capture rate for approved loans, which means both true negatives and false negatives got multiplied by 0.12.

The total monetary value was averaged across all simulated combinations of sensitivity and specificity for a specified AUC value. By running the procedure across a range of AUC values it was determined that a 0.001 difference in AUC translated to $55,000 in loss.

Assigning a monetary value to differences in RMSE was estimated using the following methodology. Given that “risk” is defined in deviation from an expected outcome, and assuming 900 financed loans per month at $18,000 per loan 0.01 RMSE translated to $162,000 in increased risk.

Disparate Impact Analysis

Disparate impact analysis is a statistical method used to assess whether there is an unintentional and adverse impact on a particular group within a population due to certain policies, practices, or decisions, especially in the context of employment, lending, or other areas governed by anti-discrimination laws. This analysis involves evaluating the outcomes of these policies across different demographic groups (i.e., race, age, and gender). The disparate impact analysis for this model involved comparing the distributions of predictions between and among different protected groups. Additionally, any features in the model that are predictive of a protected group were made explicit and the impact of each feature's removal was tested.

Explained variance, often used in the context of linear regression or principal component analysis (PCA), measures the proportion of the variance in the dependent variable (the target variable being predicted) that is explained by the independent variables or features used in the model. Interpreting explained variance involves understanding the fraction of the total variance in the target variable that the model captures. A higher explained variance indicates that the model's features can explain a larger portion of the variability observed in the target variable. For instance, if a model has an explained variance of 0.8 (80%), it means that 80% of the variance in the target variable is accounted for by the features included in the model, leaving 20% of the variance unexplained. In simpler terms, higher explained variance signifies that the model's features are more successful in capturing and explaining the variability observed in the target variable, implying a better fit of the model to the data.

Mean Absolute Error (MAE) is a metric used to measure the average absolute differences between the predicted values and the actual values in a regression problem. Interpreting MAE involves understanding the average magnitude of errors made by the model in its predictions. The MAE value represents the average of the absolute differences between the predicted values and the actual values. For example, if the MAE is 5, it means, on average, the model's predictions are off by 5 units from the actual values. In simpler terms, MAE quantifies the average deviation of the model's predictions from the ground truth. A lower MAE indicates that, on average, the model's predictions are closer to the actual values, signifying better performance in terms of prediction accuracy.

Mean Squared Error (MSE) is a metric used to measure the average of the squared differences between the predicted values and the actual values in a regression problem. Interpreting MSE involves understanding the average magnitude of squared errors made by the model in its predictions. The MSE value represents the average of the squared differences between the predicted values and the actual values. For example, if the MSE is 25, it means, on average, the square of the model's predictions' errors is 25 units. In simpler terms, MSE quantifies the average squared deviation of the model's predictions from the ground truth. A lower MSE indicates that, on average, the model's predictions are closer to the actual values, signifying better performance in terms of prediction accuracy. However, since MSE squares the errors, it might be more sensitive to outliers compared to other metrics like Mean Absolute Error (MAE).

Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) is a metric derived from the Mean Squared Error (MSE). It represents the square root of the average of the squared differences between the predicted values and the actual values in a regression problem. Interpreting RMSE involves understanding the average magnitude of errors made by the model in its predictions. The RMSE value, being the square root of MSE, provides an easily interpretable metric in the same unit as the target variable. For instance, if the RMSE is 5, it means, on average, the model's predictions are off by approximately 5 units from the actual values. In simpler terms, RMSE measures the typical magnitude of errors made by the model, considering both the size and direction of errors. A lower RMSE indicates that, on average, the model's predictions are closer to the actual values, implying better performance in terms of prediction accuracy, especially when compared across different models or parameter settings.
